Pork Adobo

Food Recipes

Description

Pork Adobo is the quintessential Filipino comfort food, celebrated as the country’s unofficial national dish for its deeply savory, tangy, and subtly sweet flavor profile. The magic of this dish lies in its simple yet powerful braising liquid, made from a balanced blend of soy sauce, vinegar, plenty of crushed garlic, whole black peppercorns, and dried bay leaves. Marbled cuts of pork—most traditionally pork belly (liempo) or shoulder (kasim)—are simmered slowly in this marinade until the meat becomes incredibly tender, allowing the savory-sour notes to penetrate all the way to the bone while the pork fat renders beautifully into the sauce.

What truly elevates Pork Adobo is the cooking technique, which often involves searing the marinated meat in hot oil before or after simmering to create delicious, caramelized edges. As the liquid reduces, the rendered pork fat merges with the soy-vinegar mixture, creating a rich, glossy, and intensely savory sauce that is unmatched when spooned over warm, steamed white rice. Because vinegar acts as a natural preservative, Adobo is a dish that famously tastes even better the next day, as the flavors continue to meld and deepen over time.

    Ingredients

    The Meat and Marinade

  • 1 kg (2.2 lbs) Pork – Use marbled cuts like pork belly (liempo), pork shoulder (kasim), or a mix of belly and ribs, cut into 2-inch chunks.
  • 1/2 cup Soy Sauce – Preferably Filipino brands like Silver Swan or Datu Puti for an authentic flavor profile
  • 1/3 cup Cane Vinegar or Coconut Vinegar – Gives the dish its signature tangy baseline.
  • 1 head Garlic – Separated into cloves, peeled, and thoroughly crushed. Adobo relies heavily on garlic.
  • 1 tsp Whole Black Peppercorns (pamintang buo) – Slightly crushed to release their aromatic oils.
  • 3 to 4 pieces Dried Bay Leaves (laurel) – Adds a subtle earthy depth.

    • For Searing and Simmering

  • 1 to 2 tbsp Cooking Oil – For searing the marinated pork.
  • 1 cup Water – To help simmer and tenderize the pork without burning the sauce.
  • 1 to 2 tsp Brown Sugar (Optional) – To balance the salt and vinegar with a touch of sweetness.

    • Optional Add-ins

  • 3 to 4 pieces Hard-Boiled Eggs – Shelled and added during the last few minutes of simmering to soak up the dark sauce.
  • 2 medium Potatoes – Peeled, quartered, and fried before adding to the stew.

    Step-by-Step

    Phase 1: Marinating (30 minutes to overnight)

  1. Combine ingredients: In a large bowl or zip-top bag, combine your pork chunks, soy sauce, crushed garlic, crushed peppercorns, and dried bay leaves.
  2. Rest: Mix well to coat the meat evenly. Cover and let it marinate in the refrigerator for at least 30 minutes. For the deepest flavor, marinate it overnight. Do not add the vinegar yet.

    3. Phase 2: Searing the Pork (10 minutes)

  3. Separate meat and liquid: Remove the pork chunks from the marinade, shaking off excess liquid. Save the leftover marinade liquid and aromatics (garlic, bay leaves)—do not throw them away!
  4. Sear the meat: Heat 1–2 tablespoons of cooking oil in a large pot over medium-high heat. Add the pork chunks in a single layer and sear for 3–5 minutes until the edges are nicely browned and caramelized.

    5. Phase 3: Braising and Simmering (40 minutes)

  5. Add marinade and water: Pour the reserved marinade liquid, garlic, peppercorns, and bay leaves into the pot with the seared pork. Add 1 cup of water.
  6. Boil and simmer: Bring the mixture to a boil. Once boiling, lower the heat to medium-low, cover the pot, and simmer for 30 minutes, or until the pork is tender.
  7. Add the vinegar (CRITICAL STEP): Pour in your vinegar. Do not stir the pot. Let the liquid come to a boil uncovered for 3–5 minutes. This allows the harsh, raw alcohol taste of the vinegar to evaporate, leaving a smooth, pleasant tang.
  8. Balance the flavor: Stir the pot gently. If using brown sugar, mix it in now to round out the sharp salt-and-sour profile.

    9. Phase 4: Reducing the Sauce (5 minutes)

  9. Thicken the sauce: If you prefer a thick, rich, oily sauce, remove the lid and turn the heat up to medium. Let the sauce reduce and thicken for about 5 minutes until the rendered pork fat separates from the dark sauce. (If using hard-boiled eggs, drop them in now to coat them in the sauce).

    10. Serve your Pork Adobo hot over a generous bed of steamed white rice.
